Buy the frame for the load and the seat for the cleaning schedule. Across the fourteen bath-safety models in this catalogue, seven publish a seat material, four publish only a seat size or a shape, and three publish nothing about the seat at all. That gap matters most in institutional use, where the seat is wiped with alcohol, chlorine or a quaternary ammonium product several times a day, and where no load rating — 150 kg, 500 lb, 770 lb — describes what the surface will look like after two years of it.

## What a disinfectant actually attacks

**Blow-moulded HDPE** is a single-piece, non-porous shell with no coating and no seam across the sitting surface. Alcohol, chlorine-releasing products and quaternary ammonium compounds do not attack the polymer itself at working concentration. What fails first is mechanical: the moulded bosses where the seat bolts to the frame, and the hinge line on flip-back versions, where repeated flexing starts a stress crack. Scratches are a second path — a scratched HDPE seat holds soil, and the colour matters here, because a white shell shows every mark that a grey or blue one hides.

**PU with drainage holes** is a foam pad with a through-drilled pattern, and the holes are the point: water that would sit under a user drains instead of pooling, which is what a long-sitting bariatric user needs. The failure mode is different from HDPE. The holes work only if they pass through the shell, not just the pad, so ask for a sectioned sample if you are buying eight hundred of them. Where the skin or the cover is cut, an open-cell foam becomes a water reservoir and the seat is finished — foam does not dry in a wet room.

**PU leather** — the flip-back backrests on LM5205LK and LM5205CK, and the seat of the LM6108F-2727 — is a polyurethane film on a knit backing. The film tolerates wiping; the seams and the stitch line do not, because thread wicks fluid under the film, and a cracked film at the fold of a flip-back mechanism cannot be repaired. In a bath environment the backrest is usually the first part to look tired on a returned unit, which is counter-intuitive when the frame is stainless steel and rated to 350 kg.

**A U-seat**, as on the LM5206K-S stool, removes the problem rather than solving it: there is no seat plane where water stands, and the opening gives perineal access for standing transfers. It is a stool, not a chair, and it is the right answer for a wet room where the user showers standing with supervision.

## The numbers the seat specification does not carry

Seat thickness is published once, on the 500 lb model: 3 cm. On the rest, "blow-moulded" is the whole description, and thickness is the variable that decides both cushioning and how long the shell survives a drop.

Height adjustment is quoted as a level count — 5 levels on the flip-back and folding frames, 6 on the HDPE chairs, 7 on the 500 lb model. Only the LM5101KD-S publishes the span behind the count, 74–86 cm. The level count is a manufacturing fact; the span is the specification, and the difference between a 5-level and a 6-level chair can be zero centimetres of range. The [seat fit guide](/en/blog/shower-chair-seat-fit-dimensions-b2b-guide/) works through the same gap from the dimension side.

Unit weight separates two chairs built on the same seat: the LM5101D is 1.9 kg with a 1.2 mm AL6063 frame, the LM3202KD is 3.2 kg with the same HDPE seat and backrest. The 1.3 kg difference is armrests and frame section, not the seat — worth knowing when a tender prices by weight.

MOQ runs from 30 to 500 and does not follow load. The 350 kg chairs start at 100 units; the 500 lb chair at 100; the 150 kg HDPE chair at 300; the leather-backrest fashion models at 500. The lowest entry point in the whole family is the LM6108F-2727 at 30 units, a 150 kg folding frame with a leather seat pan.

## Which report covers which seat

Three documents are in circulation for this family, and they cover three different lists of models.

**RoHS report RC2503200748** covers the bath chair series LM5101, LM5103, LM5104, LM3201, LM3202, LM3203, LM3004, LM3205 and LM3206, tested on model LM3200. That list includes the LM5101D, LM5103D, LM5103KD and LM3202KD. It does not include the LM5106KD, the 350 kg stainless pair, the flip-back models or the LM6108F — so a RoHS report in the file is not a RoHS report for the model you ordered.

**GPSR certificate TQT1520D368EC** covers a folding shower stool, main test model LM5108, against EN 12520:2024 and EN 1728:2012, with test report TQT1520D368E. Those are furniture seating strength standards; the certificate is a product-safety document under GPSR 2023/988, not a medical device conformity assessment, and it names one stool rather than the bath chair range.

**The MDR Declaration of Conformity** is issued per model as a Class I self-declaration, and the model has to be named on it. Behind that sits ISO 13485:2016 certificate **8623138319**, whose scope line covers Class I medical devices including bath safety seating.

The practical consequence: ask for the DoC naming the exact model, then ask for the RoHS series list and the GPSR scope in the same email. A distributor who asks "is there CE" will be handed a certificate for a shower stool and will assume it covers a 350 kg chair.

## Purchasing recommendation

For a care home fleet, standardise on the **LM5101D** — blow-moulded HDPE, 150 kg, 300-unit MOQ, no coating to fail and nothing to dry — and keep the **LM5109D-S** as the bariatric bay chair at 350 kg and 100 units. Add the **LM5106KD** where users sit for long periods: the drainage pattern and the 3 cm pad are what that user group needs, and 500 lb at 100 units is a low entry for a bariatric-rated chair.

For retail and e-commerce, the flip-back leather models sell on appearance and the LM5205LK photographs well in blue. Buy them knowing the backrest is the wear item and the MOQ is 500 — that is not a first-order quantity for a new listing.

Three lines to put into the spec sheet before the quotation: polymer name, seat thickness, and whether the drainage holes go through the shell. Two of those three are unpublished on eleven of the fourteen models, and the first seller to answer them in writing wins the technical comparison without a price argument.

## FAQ

**Is HDPE better than PU for a care home?** For daily chemical disinfection, yes — HDPE is a solid shell with nothing to absorb. PU earns its place where a user sits for long periods and drainage matters more than chemical resilience. The two are not competing for the same user.

**Do the drainage holes make a seat weaker?** They remove material from the seat plane, which is why the 500 lb rating on that model is worth checking against the frame, not the pad. Ask for the load test behind the rating rather than assuming the holes are free.

**Can the leather backrest be replaced?** It is a fitted part, not a consumable in the catalogue, so plan on the backrest as the wear item and specify it in the spare-parts discussion at order stage. A backrest that cannot be replaced turns a frame failure into a whole-chair return.
