Age bands are a proxy; handle height is the specification. A child walker that stands too tall makes the child hang from the grips and load the frame instead of the floor, and one that stands too low forces a stoop that defeats the purpose of the device. Before comparing prices, fix three numbers with your supplier: the **handle height range**, the **internal frame width**, and the **folded depth**.

## The two measurements that set the range

**Handle height at the wrist crease.** With the child standing upright, arms relaxed at the sides, the grip should sit at the crease of the wrist. That is the same rule used for adult walking frames, and it is the one measurement that cannot be averaged across a product line. A frame that is correct for a five-year-old is 8–10 cm too low for a nine-year-old.

**Growth headroom.** Between roughly four and ten years a child adds several centimetres of standing height per year. A fixed frame therefore has a service life measured in months. This is the commercial argument for the adjustable model in our range: one height-adjustable SKU, [LM2005L-S](/en/products/stainless-steel-folding-walker-children-recovery/), covers the span that would otherwise need two or three fixed sizes. Fewer SKUs means fewer size-related returns and a smaller stock commitment per market.

The third number, **folded depth**, is the one buyers forget. Pediatric walkers travel in car boots and school bags, and a frame that folds to a wide flat bundle gets left at home. Ask for the folded dimensions with the quotation, not after the sample arrives.

The carbon fiber legs on the LM2005T are worth explaining to a buyer, because the usual objection is that carbon fiber is a premium material in a price-sensitive category. Carbon fiber sits in the legs, not the whole frame. The legs are the longest unsupported span and contribute disproportionately to total weight, while the frame carries the folding stress and is better served by steel. On a child's frame that trade-off matters twice: the printed pattern is applied under the clear coat on the leg tube, so it does not lift at the edges the way a printed film does on a painted aluminium leg.

## Where a single-size frame loses money

The returns in this category are rarely about breakage. They are about the wrong height. A parent who receives a frame that stands too tall will report that the child "cannot use it", and the unit comes back through a channel that has already paid freight twice.

Three ways to reduce that:

1. **Publish the handle height range in centimetres on the product page and the carton.** Not "suitable for ages 4–10". The age band is the marketing line; the centimetre range is the specification a fitter uses.
2. **Stock the adjustable model as the default and the fixed models as the volume item.** One adjustable SKU in a clinic or school channel absorbs the sizing conversations that would otherwise become returns.
3. **Keep the grips and tips as the serviceable parts.** Grips and leg tips are the two items that wear, and both are replaceable without replacing the frame.

## The papers a Class I child walker actually needs

Walking frames are Class I medical devices in the EU, which means the manufacturer self-declares conformity under MDR 2017/745 and issues a Declaration of Conformity. It does not mean a notified body certificate exists, and a supplier who offers "CE certificate" for a Class I walker is describing the wrong document.

What you can verify from us today:

- **ISO 13485:2016, certificate 8623138319**, issued by Huarenlian Certification Service Co., Ltd. on 19 June 2025 and valid to 18 June 2028. The scope names sales and production of Class I medical devices including walking aids, bath safety seating, commode chairs and toilet safety rails. Check the number on the certification body's register and confirm the legal entity matches the business licence.
- **RoHS test reports RC2503200748 and RC2503200997.** These cover the bath chair series, not walkers. They are substance-compliance evidence under 2011/65/EU and (EU) 2015/863, and they say nothing about mechanical strength.
- **GPSR certificate TQT1520D368EC**, issued against EN 12520:2024 and EN 1728:2012. Be precise about what this is: a product-safety certificate for one folding shower stool model (LM5108, test report TQT1520D368E). EN 12520 and EN 1728 are **furniture seating** standards. If a supplier presents this certificate as strength evidence for a walker, it is the wrong document — walkers are tested to the EN ISO 11199 series, not to seating standards.

That last point costs us a selling line, and it is still the right thing to write. A buyer who discovers the mismatch after the first audit stops trusting every other number in the file.

Freight is volume, not weight. A container of walkers cubs out long before it weighs out, so the number that decides your landed cost is the carton dimension, not the unit weight. Ask for gross weight and carton size with the quotation and build the landed-cost sheet on those figures rather than on estimates.
