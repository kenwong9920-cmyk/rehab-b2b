A folding crutch is bought for two reasons: a patient has to get it home, or a dealer has to post it. Both reasons are about length, not strength. Ten of the twenty-one crutch SKUs here fold, five publish a folded dimension, and those five split into two clear camps — 45 cm on the three-section designs, 61.5 cm on the two-section one. Everything else in this comparison follows from that 16.5 cm.

## What the third section buys, and what it costs

The three-section designs fold to 45 cm, which is the length of a forearm — that is the whole sales argument for a private customer with a small flat and no storage. The two-section LM4202B folds to 61.5 cm. The 16.5 cm difference decides whether the crutch fits a standard parcel locker, a hospital bedside cabinet, or the boot of a small car without lowering a seat.

What you give up is joints. A three-section crutch has two breaks between the handle and the tip; the two-section has one. Each break is a clearance that has to stay tight, and each one is a place where a user feels movement before anyone sees it. That is a maintenance property, not a defect — but it changes who should own the product. A rental fleet that inspects every unit before it goes out can carry three-section crutches. A private buyer who folds the crutch twice a year is better served by the two-section version, which has half the mechanism to keep tight.

The frame numbers published on the LM4205 set the class: 22.2 mm main tube, 1.2 mm wall. That is the same 7/8 inch tube the walking-aid accessory trade is built around, which is why the ferrule on these crutches is a commodity part rather than a factory order. The carbon LM4401 runs a 1.2 mm carbon wall at 1.13 kg per pair, and the aluminium elbow crutch in the same family publishes 0.49 kg for a forearm model with 10 levels. Where weight is the buying criterion, the carbon pair is not automatically the lighter answer — the model matters more than the material label.

## The carton: 1.89 kg billed against 1.13 kg

Only one folding crutch publishes a full carton: the LM4401, at 44.5 × 15 × 17 cm. That is 11,347 cm³ per unit.

At the 6,000 volumetric divisor most sea and road consolidations use, the carton bills as 1.89 kg. At the 5,000 divisor common in express, it bills as 2.27 kg. The crutch pair inside weighs 1.13 kg. So on an express lane you pay for roughly twice the mass you ship, and on any lane you pay for the carton before you pay for the crutch.

Run it up to an order: 500 pairs is 5.67 m³ before the outer carton, and outer cartons add on the order of 8–12% to that. On a 40 ft container that is a rounding error. On air freight it is the entire cost structure, and it is the number to negotiate — not the gram count of the crutch.

The 61.5 cm model sits on the other side of a line that matters in parcel pricing: longest side above 60 cm. Many carriers price a long parcel differently from a standard one, and a 61.5 cm folded crutch needs a carton longer than the fold. If your channel is mail order, the three-section crutch at 45 cm is the cheaper product to ship even if it costs more per unit at the factory.

## Documents: what covers a crutch and what does not

Three of twenty-one crutch SKUs publish a load figure: 200 lb on the LM4202B, 100 kg on both versions of the LM4401. 200 lb is 90.7 kg, which is below the 100 kg the same family declares elsewhere — worth knowing before a tender compares the two as equivalents.

The document that matters in the EU is the **MDR 2017/745 Declaration of Conformity**, issued per model as a Class I self-declaration. It has to name the model, and for a family like this that means one DoC per variant, not one for the crutch range. Behind it sits ISO 13485:2016 certificate **8623138319**, whose scope line covers Class I medical devices including walking aids.

What is not on file matters just as much. The GPSR certificate in the file — TQT1520D368EC, tested to EN 12520:2024 and EN 1728:2012 — covers a folding shower stool, main test model LM5108. EN 12520 and EN 1728 are furniture seating standards. They say nothing about crutches, and no crutch in this catalogue has been tested to them. If a tender or a distributor asks for a mechanical strength report to a specific standard, that test is commissioned separately and the lead time is quoted, not assumed. The [CE marking guide](/en/blog/ce-marking-walking-aids-mdr-class-i-b2b-guide/) sets out the full document chain for Class I walking aids.

## FAQ

**Is a three-section crutch weaker than a two-section one?** Not weaker at the factory — but it has twice the joints that can loosen, and it needs checking. The published load figures, where they exist, are identical at 100 kg across both constructions.

**Why does a 45 cm crutch cost more to buy but less to ship?** Because the factory price follows parts and assembly, while the freight price follows the carton. At 44.5 cm the carton stays under the long-parcel threshold, and the volumetric weight drops with it.

**Does the 200 lb figure on the LM4202B mean it suits a lighter user?** It means the declared load is 90.7 kg, against 100 kg on the LM4401. Check the declared figure per model rather than per range — they are not interchangeable.
