A two-wheel walking frame is not a four-wheel rollator with two wheels removed. Of the six walker SKUs in this range, exactly one — LM2002-S-W1 — carries two wheels and a hand brake. The other five are folding frames with a seat, or paediatric frames, built on the same two platforms. That single difference decides who can use the product at all, and it is the one thing a catalogue photo will not tell you.

## The six SKUs, and where the wheels actually sit

The range looks like six products. It is two platforms with three variants each.

| [LM2002-S](/en/products/foldable-stainless-steel-walker-with-seat/) | Folding walker with seat | — | — | Built-in leather | Adults / seniors | 100 |

| LM2005T | Paediatric folding walker | — | — | — | Children | 100 |
| LM2005B-S | Paediatric folding walker | — | — | — | Children | 100 |
| LM2005L-S | Paediatric folding walker | — | — | — | Children | 100 |

Three adult models sit on the LM2002 frame, three paediatric models on LM2005. The suffix is the whole specification: `-W1` adds wheels, `-W1-FS` adds an extended height range on top of that frame, `-S` is the plain seat version. A purchase order that says "LM2002, 300 units" describes a frame and no configuration.

This matters commercially because the MOQ is flat at 100 per SKU across all six. It does not aggregate. Ordering three variants at 40 units each does not reach a 100-unit floor — it is three orders that each fail it. The only way to reach volume on this platform is to concentrate on one configuration.

## What a two-wheel frame asks of the user

A four-wheel rollator steers. The rear castors pivot, the user pushes with both hands, and the frame carries part of the body weight through the handlebars. A two-wheel frame does not steer. The wheels are fixed fore-and-aft, so direction is changed by lifting the front, by tipping the frame onto one wheel, or by walking a wide arc.

That is a mechanical property with a clinical consequence. To turn a two-wheel frame, the user has to unload it — take weight off the handles, lift, and place it down pointing the new way. Anyone who cannot do that unaided cannot use the product, however stable it looks standing still. Bilateral weakness, a single-sided deficit after stroke, or a grip that cannot maintain a lift are all exclusions, not preferences.

What the two-wheel configuration buys is footprint. It is narrow, it turns inside a bathroom, it goes down a 70 cm hallway, and it moves faster than a pick-up walker because the user is not lifting the whole frame to advance. Those are indoor and transitional-use advantages. For a user who walks to a shop and needs to sit down halfway, a frame with a seat and no wheels is the better answer, and it is already in the range as LM2002-S.

LM2002-S-W1-FS is the tall-adult build on the same frame. Handle height is the specification that generates returns, because a frame 5 cm too low forces a stoop a user will not tolerate for a week. Confirm handle height in centimetres on the order line, not the word "tall".

## The brake is the part that fails

LM2002-S-W1 is the only SKU in the six that publishes a brake at all: hand-operated. In this class of product that means a Bowden cable — an inner wire running inside an outer conduit, pulling a brake block against the wheel or a friction pad against the tyre. German maintenance buyers search for it under its own name, *Bowdenzug*, which tells you it is treated as a wear part rather than a frame component.

| Brake type | Where it appears | What wears | What it means for the order |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | LM2002-S, LM2005T/B-S/L-S | Nothing — no brake exists | Frame must be lifted to stop; unsuitable on a slope |
| Hand-operated Bowden cable | LM2002-S-W1 | Inner wire, conduit ends, brake block | Spare cables and blocks must ship with the first order |
| Drum or disc brake | Not in this range | Pads, cable tension | Higher unit cost, needs a service procedure |

The cable is the only part on the frame with an internal moving surface, and it is the only part that can be damaged while the product sits in a warehouse — a kink in the conduit from a badly stacked carton shows up as a brake that drags three months later. Two consequences for a purchase order. First, specify the spare cable and brake block as line items on the first order; a distributor who has to source a cable separately pays more for the part than for the margin on the frame. Second, ask how the frame is packed, because a folded frame with the cable looped across the hinge is the failure mode.

Only one of the six SKUs has a brake. If the product will be sold into a market where users walk outdoors or on inclines, that one SKU is the only one that can be offered without a written warning, and it is the model the [walkers category](/en/products/category/walkers/) is built around.

## The load figure none of the six publishes

Here is the uncomfortable number. Across all six walker SKUs, the count of published maximum load values is zero. Not a floor, not a range — the field is empty in every one, while the same catalogue publishes 100 kg on eight walking canes and 150 kg on four commode chairs.

A buyer who fills that gap with a category assumption is guessing. The relevant standard for this product class is EN ISO 11199-2, which covers rollators and sets static strength and fatigue requirements, but a standard existing is not the same as a report existing for your model. What the range does hold is the quality system certificate — ISO 13485:2016, number 8623138319, issued by Huarenlian, valid to 2028-06-18, with a scope that names walking aids, bath safety seating, commode chairs and toilet safety rails. That certificate tells you how the factory is run. It does not certify the load capacity of any individual frame, and no certificate of that kind can substitute for a model-specific test report.

Put the target load on the purchase order as a requirement and ask for the report against it before the deposit. Written on the order line it becomes a specification the factory has to meet; left as a catalogue assumption it becomes the first thing a distributor discovers after a return. The carbon and aluminium ranges elsewhere in the catalogue show what disclosure looks like — tube diameters, wall thicknesses, folded dimensions — and the walker range does not yet carry it. Treat the absence as a question, not a number to infer.

## MOQ, price band and lead time

All six SKUs carry a 100-unit MOQ and a 15–30 day delivery window, which is the most uniform block in the catalogue. The published price band for the category is US$12–20 FOB. The uniformity follows from the shared platforms: no low-volume variant exists, because there is no separate tooling to amortise on the paediatric frame. What is not uniform is what ships with each unit — a braked frame carries a cable, a lever and a block the plain version does not, and those parts change the carton as well as the unit cost. Ask for packed dimensions per configuration, not per category.

## Purchasing recommendation

1. **Decide the wheel configuration before the quantity.** Two wheels is an indoor, narrow-footprint, unload-to-turn product. If the end user cannot lift the frame while standing, the correct SKU is LM2002-S, not a cheaper wheeled version.
2. **Write the model suffix on the PO.** LM2002 alone is a frame family, not a product. `-W1` and `-W1-FS` are different products with different height ranges.
3. **Do not aggregate the MOQ.** 100 units is per SKU. Three configurations at 40 units is three failed minimums, not one passed one.
4. **Order spare brake cables and blocks with the first shipment** on the wheeled SKU. They are the only wear parts on the frame and they cost less than the freight to send them later.
5. **Get the load figure in writing.** Nothing in this range publishes one, and EN ISO 11199-2 being the applicable standard does not mean a report exists for your model. Ask before the deposit, not after the first return.

## FAQ

**Is a two-wheel rollator easier to steer than a four-wheel one?**
No. Fixed wheels mean the frame has to be lifted or tipped to change direction. Four-wheel rollators steer with rear castors and need only a push.

**Can a two-wheel rollator be used outdoors?**
On a flat, firm surface. Without a parking brake it is not safe on an incline, and the fixed wheels catch on gravel and thresholds.

**What load can these frames carry?**
None of the six publishes a figure. Ask for a model-specific test report against your target load; do not apply a category default.
