A cane and a crutch are not two versions of the same product at different prices. A cane takes a share of load off one leg and gives the user a balance point; a crutch takes load off the leg entirely and puts it into the hand, the forearm or — wrongly — the armpit. That single difference, the user's weight-bearing status, decides the SKU. Age decides nothing, and neither does frame material, which is why a range built on "one cane, one crutch, both cheap" ends up with two dead lines.

## The question that decides the SKU

Physiotherapy works with five weight-bearing categories: non-weight-bearing, touchdown, partial, weight-bearing as tolerated, and full. Two are cane territory, three are crutch territory.

**Cane.** For a user who can stand on the affected leg but should not load it fully, or who needs a balance point after a stroke, a knee replacement or a general strength loss. Standard teaching holds the cane in the hand opposite the affected side. A single-point cane is a balance aid and a partial unloader; a quad cane stands on its own when the user lets go, which matters in a bathroom, and it is the model prescribed after a stroke rather than after a sprain.

**Crutch.** For non-weight-bearing and touchdown weight-bearing, where the affected foot carries nothing or almost nothing. The axillary crutch is the short-term post-trauma device: cheap, wide adjustment range, and the one most often fitted incorrectly. The forearm crutch is the long-term device — stairs, slopes, a free hand for a door — and it is what a user on crutches for months should be given. A pharmacy that stocks only canes loses every post-operative discharge; one that stocks only axillary crutches sends home a stroke patient who needs a quad cane.

A cane that folds to 34 cm fits a handbag, which is the whole reason the four-section models exist. A crutch that folds to 44.5 cm fits a day bag; the LM4202B at 61.5 cm does not, and a buyer who assumed otherwise has created a return. The nine-to-one weight gap between a 120 g cane and a 1.13 kg crutch pair is also a carton gap — canes ship in 35×16×7.5 cm boxes, crutches take 44.5×15×17 cm per pair — and that is where landed cost diverges from FOB.

## What 100 kg and 200 lb actually say

This is the trap in the category, and it catches experienced buyers.

The cane models publish **100 kg**. The carbon forearm crutch publishes **100 kg per pair**. The LM4202B publishes **200 lb**. Those are not the same tier: 200 lb is 90.7 kg, *below* the 100 kg the other models state. A catalogue that lists all three as "heavy duty" is not comparing like with like, and a tender specifying "minimum 100 kg" excludes the 200 lb model without anyone noticing.

First rule: ask for the basis — per unit or per pair, static or fatigue, to which standard. One cane here references **MZ/T 199-2023**, a Chinese industry standard for walking sticks, which is worth more in a technical file than a bare number because it says what was tested.

Second rule: treat an empty cell as information. Sixteen canes and nineteen crutches state no load at all. The honest answer to "what does it hold" for those is not a copied figure but "the sheet does not state it, so we will load-test the model you select before you commit" — worth more in a tender than a number nobody can defend.

Third rule: a load figure is not a fitting rule. A 100 kg cane set 5 cm too high is worse than a 90 kg cane set correctly, because the failure mode is a wrist, not a bent tube.

## Documents a walking-aid order needs

Walking aids sit in Class I under EU MDR 2017/745, which means the manufacturer declares conformity itself. There is no notified-body certificate behind a cane or a crutch, for us or for any competitor in this class — a supplier who produces one for a walking stick is describing a different product.

**ISO 13485:2016, certificate 8623138319**, Huarenlian Certification Service, valid to 2028-06-18. Read the scope line: it names Class I medical devices including walking aids. A QMS certificate for another product family does not cover this order.

**A Declaration of Conformity under MDR 2017/745, per model**, supplied with the document package rather than published — and it names the models it covers. A model not named on it is not covered by it. **FDA establishment registration and device listing** is separate again, and it is not approval: the correct wording is registered and listed, and listing is per product, so confirm the model and product code.

One document that does *not* travel with a cane order: our GPSR certificate TQT1520D368EC is issued against EN 12520:2024 and EN 1728:2012 — furniture seating standards — for a folding shower stool, model LM5108. Those standards do not apply to a cane, and forwarding that certificate for a walking-aid order is a gamble on the buyer not reading it. What does apply is design protection: the carbon fibre cane shape carries **EUIPO registered Community design 015134442-0001** — the document that lets a private label range be built on that silhouette.

## Purchasing recommendation

For a pharmacy or home-care range, take three SKUs, not eight.

One **height-adjustable cane** covering 72–96.5 cm, because that single model serves the largest group — the balance and partial-weight-bearing user — and clears MOQ at 200 units. One **quad cane** for the post-stroke user who needs the aid to stand on its own. One **crutch pair**, and here the choice is clinical: axillary for post-trauma and short-term, forearm for rehabilitation and long-term. If you cannot decide, take the forearm pair — it is the model that survives month three, and the 200 lb versus 100 kg gap above tells you which to order.

Put four lines in the PO: the height range in centimetres your market needs, the load rating with its basis per model, the folded length, and the tip and ferrule as a spare-part line. Then check the range on the [walking canes category page](/en/products/category/walking-canes/) and, if crutches join the same order, the [crutches category page](/en/products/category/crutches/). MOQ is per model and per colour, not per order, so a three-model first container is a normal way to start.

## FAQ

**Can one cane serve both partial weight-bearing and balance-only users?** Yes, if its height range covers your market. What changes the SKU is whether the user needs the aid to stand upright on its own — that is the quad cane, not the single-point.

**Is 100 kg enough for a walking cane in Europe?** For the overwhelming majority of users, yes, and 100 kg is the figure most of this range states. The relevant question is not the number but the basis, and whether the model you are buying is the model that was tested. Ask for the report, not the badge.

**Do canes and crutches need a notified body certificate?** No. Both are Class I devices, self-declared under MDR 2017/745. A tender requiring a notified body certificate for a walking stick is asking for a document that cannot exist for this device class, and the answer is a Declaration of Conformity naming the model.
