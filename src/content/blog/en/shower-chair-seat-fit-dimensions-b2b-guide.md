A shower chair fails on fit before it fails on load. Across fourteen bath-safety records in this catalogue, nine publish a load figure and only three publish a seat size — yet the seat is what decides whether the user stays seated. Seat width, seat depth and seat height above the floor are the three numbers to settle first; the frame material is a consequence of them.

## Three numbers, and who publishes them

Published seat dimensions in this family are 42.5 × 32.5 cm on the LM5106KD, 38 × 40 cm on the LM5103D and 51 × 40 cm on the LM5101KD-S. That 13 cm spread in width is the whole argument. A 38 cm seat fits a narrow user and a narrow wet room; a 51 cm seat is the one that clears a 130 kg user's hips without pressing on the trochanters, and it needs 13 cm more floor clearance.

Width is measured across the widest part of the hips plus 2–5 cm of clearance. Under that, the user shifts weight to one side to avoid the seat edge, and a chair that is used for ten minutes a day becomes a chair that is used for two. Depth matters in the opposite direction: a seat deeper than the user's thigh length pushes the back of the knee against the front edge, which is the pressure point that ends a shower early.

The height question is the one buyers get wrong, and the catalogue is honest about why.

## A level count is not a height

Seven models in this family publish a number of height levels: five, six or seven. One model publishes a range in centimetres, 74–86 cm on the LM5101KD-S, and six publish nothing at all in the height column. Five levels and six levels tell you how many holes are drilled; they do not tell you the span, and without the span you cannot say whether the top setting serves a 190 cm user or whether the bottom setting serves a 150 cm one.

That is a specification question, not a marketing one, and it belongs in writing at quotation: what is the seat height above the floor at the lowest and highest setting, and what is the increment between them. A six-level chair spanning 11 cm moves 2.2 cm per step, which keeps almost every user within one step of correct. A five-level chair spanning the same 11 cm moves 2.75 cm per step, and the users at the extremes end up on the last hole with their heels off the floor.

The fitting rule does not depend on the chair. Measure the user seated, feet flat, from the floor to the crease behind the knee — the popliteal height. A seat at that height, or one to two centimetres below it, gives a flat foot, a knee near 90° and a thigh roughly level. For an adult that lands in the mid-forties of centimetres; for a bariatric user it is often higher, and a chair that only reaches 45 cm will leave that user's knees above their hips.

## Where the seat meets the certificate

Seat material decides more than comfort, because it decides which document describes the product. The RoHS report for the bath chair series, **RC2503200748**, is written against test model LM3200 and names the series it covers: LM5101, LM5103, LM5104, LM3201, LM3202, LM3203, LM3004, LM3205, LM3206. A model inside that list is covered. A new seat compound is a question, and the honest answer is that the substance evidence may no longer describe the article.

The GPSR certificate **TQT1520D368EC**, issued under EN 12520:2024 and EN 1728:2012, is narrower than it looks: it covers a folding shower stool, main test model LM5108, and states on its face that it applies to the tested sample. Those two standards are furniture seating standards — strength and durability of the seat — and they are the right evidence for a bath chair, provided nobody relabels them as a medical device assessment. The medical device document is the Declaration of Conformity, issued per model under MDR 2017/745 as a Class I self-declaration, alongside **ISO 13485:2016 certificate 8623138319** for the quality system.

Three practical consequences for a buyer specifying a seat:

- **Drainage holes or a solid seat is a hygiene decision.** The 42.5 × 32.5 cm PU seat on the LM5106KD carries drainage holes; blow-moulded HDPE seats are solid. In a communal shower, drainage shortens drying time between users; in a single household, a solid seat is easier to wipe.
- **Armrest width is the bariatric constraint, not seat width.** The LM5106KD uses figure-8 foam armrests with adjustable width. If the armrests are fixed and the hips are wide, the user enters from the front or not at all.
- **Backrest height decides who can lean back.** The same model publishes a 38.5 × 19.5 cm backrest with two positions; a low backrest supports the lumbar spine, a high one supports the thorax. Neither is better — they serve different transfer techniques.

## What to put in the enquiry

Six lines, and a quotation comes back comparable instead of decorative. Seat height at the lowest and highest setting, with the increment. Seat width and depth. Load rating and the standard behind it. Whether the frame is 1.2 mm or 1.4 mm aluminium, or stainless. Armrest type and whether width is adjustable. MOQ for the colour you actually want, since colour and MOQ are quoted together.

Lead time on the family is **15–30 days from order confirmation**, and the published FOB band for bath safety is **US$10–15 per unit** at standard specification. Compare two suppliers at the same seat size and the same load rating, not at the same headline price — the [bath safety category](/en/products/category/bath-safety/) is where the fourteen models sit side by side, and the [MOQ and pricing page](/en/moq-pricing/) states what the band is quoted against.

## Recommendation

Fix the seat before the frame. If you are buying for a care home or a rental fleet, take the published seat size as the filter, then check the load rating against the heaviest user you actually serve rather than the average — the 350 kg models cost more per unit and cost less per placement because they serve every resident in the building.

If your volume is under 100 units, you are choosing between the four models at 100–200 MOQ, and that is a legitimate constraint rather than a negotiation problem. If your volume is above 300, the flip-back and leather-backrest models become available, and the question moves to replacement parts and how easily the seat is cleaned between users. Either way, ask for the seat height span in centimetres in writing. A supplier who cannot answer that has not read their own specification, and a chair that misses the popliteal height comes back for a reason unrelated to quality.

## FAQ

**Is a higher load rating always the right choice for a care home?** Yes, within reason. A 350 kg chair serves a 60 kg resident as safely as a 150 kg chair does, and it removes the need to hold a second SKU for bariatric placements. The cost is unit price and weight — the stainless models are heavier to move between rooms.

**How many height settings do I need?** Enough that the increment is under 3 cm. With a published span you can calculate it; without one, ask. Six or seven levels over a 12–14 cm span is a working range for a mixed population, and it is the reason those models exist.

**Does the GPSR certificate cover the whole bath-safety family?** No. It covers the folding shower stool tested as LM5108 under EN 12520:2024 and EN 1728:2012. For other models the documents to ask for are the RoHS report covering the series and the per-model Declaration of Conformity.
